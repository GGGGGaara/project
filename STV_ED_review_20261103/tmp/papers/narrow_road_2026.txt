     Abstract
     This study proposes a risk-predictive path planning and control framework for autonomous driving (AD) and
     advanced driver-assistance systems (ADAS) operating on narrow urban roads with limited visibility. When
     driving through unsignalized intersections, buildings or  parked vehicles often create blind spots where
     pedestrians or other road users may suddenly dart out , becoming a significant safety risk. To address this
     challenge, this research defines a method to simultaneously determine the safe speed and safe lateral distance
     based on the geometric relationship between the ego vehicl  e and the occluded area. The safe speed is defined as
     the maximum velocity at which a collision can be a voided through emergency braking, while the lateral gap
     ensures avoidance of side-faced colli sions. Using these risk-predictive pa rameters, a non-linear polynomial path
     generation method is formulated, in which cubic and quar  tic functions describe the vehicle’s speed profile and
     trajectory, respectively. The coefficients of these polynomia  ls are optimized to minimize deviations from desired
     paths while preventing the vehicle from entering the predicted high-risk zones by the proposed method.
     Comprehensive MATLAB simulations were performed usi  ng a 3-DoF vehicle model to validate the proposed
     method. Results show that the framework successfully generated smooth and safe trajectories, allowing the
     vehicle to avoid potential collisions under various road widths                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             and darting-out conditions. Even in constrained
     road scenarios, the system maintained stability and a  voided frontal collisions. The proposed method effectively
     balances safety and mobility, demonstrating strong adapta  bility for future integration into real-world AD/ADAS
     systems.

1. Introduction

     In recent years, extensive research has been conducted on path planning and speed profiles generation to realize
Autonomous Driving (AD) and Advanced Driver Assistance Systems (ADAS) in urban environments (González et al.,
2016; Paden et al., 2016; Reda et al., 2024). Driving on narrow roads in mixed traffic with vulnerable road users (VRUs)
presents particular challenges for autonomous driving                      vehicles (Wang et al., 2022; Koç                et al., 2021). When drivers
navigate through unsignalized intersections on narrow roads, there is a risk of unexpected darting out of other road users,
such as pedestrians, bicycles, motorbikes, or cars. This risk is further highlighted when crossing roads are occluded by
objects such as buildings and parked vehicles                   (Wang et al., 2023). To avoid or mitigate collisions with objects that
suddenly emerge from blind areas, human drivers and the AD/ADAS systems must decelerate to a safe speed through
appropriate risk prediction (Wang et al., 2022; Brycht, 2020; Lin et al., 2023). For AD/ADAS vehicles to safely pass
through intersections, they must decelerate in advance, but as slowly as possible. However, users often find such cautious
behavior unacceptable due to perceived loss of comfort or mobility (Wang et al., 2023; Ito et al., 2022).
     Observations of careful and competent                   human      drivers show that they maintain sufficient lateral distance from
occlusions, allowing them to reduce excessive deceleration. Our previous work defined safe speeds for intersection right-
turns to balance safety and mobility, based on geometric relationships between the ego vehicle and occluding objects.
However,      this   earlier   research  focused        solely    on  intersection  right-turn  scenarios  and  predicted  smooth,  feasible

[PDF PAGE 2]
trajectories (Fujinami and Raksincharoensak, 2021; Fujinami et al., 2021; Fujinami, 2021).
     This study expands the scope beyond intersection turning to include driving through intersections, negotiating curves,
interacting with surrounding dynamic objects, and overtaking parked vehicles. Our research is to define safe speeds and
appropriate lateral gaps in       risk-predictive scenarios that ensure both safety and high mobility in the residential area
involving occluded dynamic VRUs and vehicles.
     Focusing  on      straight   driving  on  urban  narrow  roads,  this  paper  proposes              a   risk  prediction    framework  that
simultaneously determines safe speed and lateral gap to avoid potential darting-out objects from blind spots. Furthermore,
this paper introduces a path generation method that enables the vehicle to navigate smoothly without entering predicted
high-risk areas by solving an optimization problem. The effectiveness of our proposed method is demonstrated through
comprehensive simulation studies.

2. Risk definitions

     This study aims to reduce collisions with the dynamic objects that may dart out from behind stationary obstacles in
scenarios such as intersections with poor visibility, overtaking parked vehicles, and                        similar urban situations. Unlike
conventional risk definitions based on probability and damage severity, the proposed framework predicts                                   potential
darting-out risk in a deterministic manner by identifying reachable collision regions of virtual darting                       -out objects under
conservative assumptions. For clarity and simplicity, we first introduce a representative scenario in which the ego vehicle
drives straight through an unsignalized intersection with poor visibility. Although this paper focuses on this specific case,
the proposed method can be extended to other scenarios by appropriately setting the parameters that define risk. The
geometry of the target intersection is shown in Fig. 1. The intersection is a T-junction where a narrow side road merges
perpendicularly from the left onto the main road. A potential object may dart out from the side road and cross the main
lane.

     A key issue when a vehicle passes through an unsignalized intersection with poor visibility is whether the driver or
system can avoid a collision          by deceleration if an object suddenly darts out from the blind                     area. Drivers typically
decelerate to an appropriate speed to anticipate such sudden crossing. However, if a darting out object strikes the side-
face of the ego vehicle, the collision cannot be avoided merely by braking, even when equipped with an Autonomous
Emergency Braking System (AEBS). On the other hand, maintaining a sufficient lateral distance from the occluded area
can prevent side-face collisions. Accordingly, this section defines two primary risk factors for blind-spot scenarios: safe
speed and safe lateral gap. The following subsections introduce these risk factors and discuss their characteristics.

2.1 Safe speed definitions
     Previous studies defined safe speed as the speed at which an ego vehicle can stop via emergency braking before
reaching a predicted collision point. However, this definition assumes a full stop and does not allow the ego vehicle to
cross the potential trajectory of a darting-out object. To address this issue, earlier research introduced a virtual darting-
out object and used a time-to-collision (TTC)               threshold to evaluate visible distance at blind            spots. When the       visible
distance exceeds a critical TTC (tcrit),         the system does not intervene, because the darting-out object would also have
sufficient time to avoid a collision. Therefore, safe speed guarantees collision avoidance particularly when visibility at
blind spots is limited     (Fujinami and Raksincharoensak, 2021              ; Fujinami et al., 2021; Fujinami, 2021). In this paper, a
bicycle is considered as a representative virtual darting-out object, and tcrit = 1.5 s is adopted as a sufficient time margin
for collision avoidance. This value represents the time required for a cyclist to perceive an approaching vehicle and

[PDF PAGE 3]
complete an avoidance or stopping maneuver under typical urban conditions.

virtual object darting out from an occluded area, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The ego vehicle is assured to be equipped with
an object detection sensor located at the center of the front face of the vehicle. This research extends the concept to
straight-driving scenarios at perpendicular intersections. Given the visibility at blind spot and the assumed darting-out
trajectory, we can express the dart-out distance Dcrit at TTC of virtual object tcrit and assumed darting-out speed of the

     However, if the safe speed were strictly enforced until the ego vehicle reaches the predicted dart-out trajectory, the

through the intersection. Therefore, a termination condition for the safe-speed-based control is required.
     In this study, once the visible distance exceeds Dcrit, that is, when the ego vehicle reaches the curve y = fcrit(x), the
occluded area is considered to be sufficiently observable, and there is no risk in terms of frontal collision risk. Beyond
this curve, a darting-out object moving at a speed lower than vvir cannot collide with the front face of the ego vehicle by
definition.

     Nevertheless, even after the ego vehicle passes the curve y = fcrit(x), a residual risk of side-face collision may still
exist if a darting-out object emerges and moves with constant velocity. In such cases, although increasing tcrit provides
the darting-out object with sufficient time for evasive action, it is difficult for the ego vehicle itself to actively avoid side-
face collisions solely by braking.            Therefore, the next subsection introduces an additional geometric condition to
determine the optimal position (xcrit, ycrit) that enables the ego vehicle to avoid both frontal and side-face collisions.

2.2 Safe lateral gap definitions
     By maintaining a sufficient lateral distance from blind spots, the ego vehicle can guarantee avoidance of side-face
collisions and safely traverse occluded scenarios without collision risk. To determine the lateral gap for the ego vehicle
passing through the intersection, we defined the possible darting-out distance from blind areas. Figure 3 illustrates the
possible darting-out area of virtual objects. The green trapezoidal region represents the area that a virtual object can reach
at vehicle speed vego(t). Avoiding this trapezoidal area ensures prevention of side-face collisions with predefined virtual
darting-out objects.



















                                   Fig. 3     Schematic definitions of darting-out area for virtual object.

     Figure 4 shows the relationship between darting-out areas and safe speeds with respect to vehicle speed vego(t). In
this figure, the red line indicates curve y = fcrit(x); however, the safe speed on this curve varies with positions. Therefore,
we can represent a safe-speed curve for position (xcrit, ycrit) in three-dimensional space. Vehicles must travel at speeds
lower than the defined safe-speeds surface, meaning the vehicles cannot pass through the red shaded regions.
     To ensure both safety and mobility, the system should select the highest feasible speed with minimal lateral deviation.
As shown in Fig. 4, we can simultaneously define the optimal safe speed vsafe and lateral position ycrit. If other road users
or the limited road width restricts available lateral gaps and the ego vehicle needs to enter the green area, our method
selects the maximum possible gap. Even in such cases, the vehicle can still avoid the frontal collision with the darting-
out objects.

3. Non-linear polynomial path planning

     The most significant advantage of this method is its high adaptability, allowing for the                              easy and simultaneous
determination of both safe speed and safe lateral distance. By using the derived position (xcrit, ycrit) and the safe speed vsafe
as waypoints for path planning,            the   vehicle path     is  generated     by optimizing      the   coefficients of     cubic and     quartic
polynomial functions, which determine the ego vehicle’s speed profile and trajectory curve, respectively. Huy Do (2015).
adopted a similar approach, utilizing curves defined by polynomial functions. That research expressed these functions as
time-dependent, whereas the present study defines them as functions of position to facilitate waypoint adaptation. The
objective of these functions is to ensure that the resulting curves do not enter restricted regions defined by factors such
as speed limits, safe speeds, road boundaries, and potential darting-out areas. Figure 5 shows example curves of the speed
profile and trajectory. The longitudinal position, speed, and lateral position at the terminal point of the curves are denoted
as Lp, vLp, and yLp respectively. These paths are repeatedly generated at each control sampling time to compute the vehicle
control commands.

4.2 Simulations
4.2.1 Preparation
     The simulations were conducted using MATLAB to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed risk prediction
method and simultaneous optimization-based path generation method. An autonomous vehicle was simulated to pass
through a narrow blind T-intersection, where the crossing had a width of 5.0 m. It was a two-way narrow road without
center lines, and the speed limit was set to 30 km/h. The simulation adopted a 3-DoF point mass vehicle model. This
model simplifies the vehicle motion            into yaw dynamics and          longitudinal    and   lateral  directions.     The vehicle was
controlled to follow the target acceleration profile and trajectory curve introduced in the previous sections. The ego
autonomous vehicle was equipped with an AEBS that imitates the functionality of a conventional AEBS. The system
activates emergency brake when a moving object collides with the front face of the ego vehicle under the assumption
that both objects maintain uniform linear motion, and when the TTC of the ego vehicle becomes shorter than a predefined
threshold   ttcEB. When the emergency brake is applied, the                 vehicle    decelerates with      a constant jerk     jEB_lim  and its
acceleration is saturated at aEB. Table 1 presents the configurations of the vehicle model, the autonomous driving system,

4.2.2 Vehicle navigation and control simulations
     A vehicle automatically drove through the course without any driver input. In this simulation, three road widths were
prepared: 4.0 m, 6.0 m, and 8.0 m. Risk-predictive autonomous driving simulations were conducted under each of these
conditions in MATLAB. The simulation calculations were performed in a real-time environment with average update
frequencies 130.3 Hz (Intel Core i9-10900K CPU @ 3.70GHz, RAM 32GB, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3070).
     Figure 6 (a) and (b) show the timelapse snapshots of the bird              -eye-view of the simulations. The results indicated that
our proposed method controlled the vehicle to take an appropriate lateral gap and decelerate in advance,                            allowing the
vehicle to effectively avoid the      collision with the darting-out object. On the other hand, the vehicle without the                 proposed
method could not avoid the collision even with AEBS engagement.
     Figure 7 (a) to (d) show the simulations results under three road-width                    conditions. As shown in the figures, the
proposed system successfully generated safe speed and trajectory profiles simultaneously, while avoiding high-risk areas
with moderate and smooth acceleration and steering maneuvers across all the road widths. Under the narrow roads
conditions (4.0 m and 6.0 m), the road did not provide sufficient space to avoid entering the predicted dart-out area (green

Conversely, under the wider road conditions (8.0 m) in Fig. 7 (c), sufficient space was available to maintain distance

4.2.3 Collision avoidance performance evaluation for darting-out pedestrian
     To evaluate the collision-avoidance performance                  against darting-out      pedestrians, we conducted comprehensive
simulations under various darting-out conditions. Road widths ranging from 4.0 to 9.0 m were prepared, and a pedestrian
was set to cross the intersection at predetermined timings. The darting-out timing, referred to as the “time offset,” was
varied from     −3.0 to 11.0 seconds       , calibrated   such that    0.0 seconds     corresponds to the       moment     when the darting      -out
object  would collide with       the front -left corner   of the ego vehicle      if neither AEB nor safe spee       d/lateral gap control were
applied. As the time offset increases, the object darts out at a later timing.
     Figure 8 shows the collision-avoidance results of the comprehensive simulations. The results revealed that no cases
involving front-face collisions of the ego vehicle collided were observed, since all such cases were avoided by AEB
engagement. Next, 200 collisions occurred out of 5781 simulation cases. In these cases, the darting-out object collided
with the side-face of the ego vehicle, which cannot be prevented solely through AEB activation. However, 197 of the 200
collisions occurred with a TTC of 1.5 s or more, measured at the moment the darting-out became visible to the ego
vehicle’s sensor position (TTC-visible). Only 3 cases had a TTC-visible shorter than 1.5 s, with the minimum TTC-
visible being 1.4 s. From the simulation parameters, avoiding entry into the collision-risk area required the ego vehicle
to maintain a lateral distance greater than 7.4 m, and the simulation results also indicated that no collision occurred at
road width exceeding 7.4 m. From those results, the proposed risk-prediction and control method successfully navigated
the vehicle to safe states and effectively avoided collisions across a wide range of road width and darting-out timing.

4.2.4 Effect of prediction accuracy on darting-out speed parameter
     This subsection examines how the prediction accuracy of the virtual dart-out speed vvir affects the collision-avoidance
performance of the proposed method. By fixing the road width as 7.0 m, we conducted a comprehensive simulation in
which the predicted dart-out speed of the virtual object and the actual dart-out speed were independently varied from 1.0
to 10.0 m/s, and the dart-out timing was set from             −3.0 to 11.0 seconds.
     Figure 9 shows the simulation results. When the predicted dart-out speed vvir was higher than the actual speed vobj,
the number of collision cases increased, but all TTC-visible values were greater than 1.5 s. This is because a higher
predicted  dart-out  speed  yields  a  lower  calculated  safe  speed,  resulting  in  slower  vehicle  traversal  through  the
intersection. Although this increased the number of collision cases, it also provides a longer TTC for the darting-out
object. On the other hand, when the predicted dart-out speed vvir was lower than the actual speed vobj, some collisions
occurred with TTC-visible values shorter than 1.5 s. These results indicated that the accuracy of the predicted dart-out
speed affects collision-avoidance performance; however, overestimating the dart-out speed does not significantly degrade
system safety. Therefore, the virtual dart-out speed parameter allows the system to be configured with different levels of
conservativeness under predefined safety constraints. Ideally, the virtual dart-out speed should be set to accurately reflect

[PDF PAGE 13]
actual darting-out behavior; however, achieving such accuracy would require sufficient statistical observations of darting-
out characteristics in occluded environments, as well as a reliable theoretical model to describe this behavior, both of
which remain challenging under current knowledge.

5. Conclusions

     This paper focused on the straight-road driving on urban narrow roads and proposed risk prediction and continuous
path generation methods         that  achieve    safe vehicle control for autonomous driving and advanced driver                       assistance
systems.   The proposed framework            enables   autonomous vehicles         to safely navigate scenarios involving blind spots,
balancing safety and driving comfort.
     The main contributions of this research are summarized as follows:
     -    Theoretical  definitions  of  safe  speed  and          lateral  distance   that   guarantee    collision  avoidance      through    the
          emergency avoidance system.
     -    Comprehensive simulations demonstrating that the proposed method effectively avoided darting-out events.
     -    Polynomial-based curve generation method enabling the autonomous vehicle to navigate safely without entering
          collision-risk area.
     Simulation results confirmed that the proposed framework can simultaneously determine safe speed and lateral
distance in real time, ensuring both safety and mobility in narrow urban environments.
     Compared with existing occlusion-aware approaches such as potential field-based methods and Responsibility-
Sensitive Safety, the proposed method formulates safety using a geometric risk-prediction framework with closed-form
computation. This enables efficient real-time evaluation of safety constraints while maintaining deterministic safety
assurance in narrow urban environments. The proposed approach is particularly suitable for practical autonomous driving
and advanced driver assistance systems and can serve as a complement to existing safety functionalities in autonomous
driving applications. Furthermore, the proposed algorithm can be integrated into existing AD/ADAS architectures using
onboard sensors and vehicle control units without requiring additional hardware.
     Future work will extend the proposed method to more complex road geometries, including curved roads, multiple
blind spots, and dynamic traffic participants, and validate its performance through Vehicle-in-the-Loop experiments.
